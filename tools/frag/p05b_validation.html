Statistical methods, and why each

Paired seeds (common random numbers)

Every planner sees the same crowds: same seed, same start, goal and people (DEFENSE Q12; run_closed.py runs seed-outer, planner-inner, amendment 2). curve_compare.load() keeps only seeds present in every arm, so a run cut short still gives paired comparisons. Why (our rationale): without pairing, crowd-to-crowd variation adds to every difference; with the same crowds it cancels, and the power calculation runs on paired SDs (D-14). Backing: REFERENCES #21 says plainly that no paper we checked prescribes paired seeds: "That is standard common-random-numbers practice and our own design choice, so state it as a choice." For common random numbers as a variance-reduction technique, REFERENCES now cites Law 2015 [42]. Francis et al. 2025 [25] recommend comparing policies on the same scenarios. The T1 intervals were marginal per configuration, not paired, which is conservative (DEFENSE Q12).

Person-level bootstrap (offline)

Intervals on offline differences resample whole people, not rows. Why: "rows 0.4 s apart are near-copies" (docs/CTO-REVIEW.md §3), so a row bootstrap would treat one person's near-identical rows as independent observations and give intervals that are too narrow (claim A has 324 rows from 195 people going to ETH, RESULTS). GATES §2 requires it. How: tools/claim_a_boot.py computes the per-row difference (TabPFN minus LightGBM, averaged over the 5 context seeds), sums it per person (episode, agent_id), draws persons with replacement 5,000 times and takes the ratio of summed differences to summed row counts; 95% percentile interval. The intent gate uses the same scheme with 2,000 draws (tools/intent_layer.py docstring). The bootstrap itself is Efron 1979 [38]. Agarwal et al. 2021 [33] back bootstrap interval estimates for few-run comparisons; REFERENCES notes they use a stratified bootstrap over runs and tasks, not ours.

Paired-seed bootstrap (closed loop)

Seeds are resampled with replacement, the same draw for every arm, and the whole comparison is recomputed per draw: 2,000 draws in curve_compare.compare(), 5,000 in inloop_claimb.py; 95% percentile intervals. In curve_compare, a draw in which the comparison can't be made (the baseline point falls outside the front and no dominance exists) counts as −∞, i.e. against the planner under test. The one-sided p-value is the share of draws with difference ≤ 0.

Matched-safety curves on the Pareto front

A single operating point can't separate "safer" from "slower" (CTO-REVIEW §3), and the original absolute rule (≤ 2% collisions, ≤ 1 s intrusion) "disqualifies baselines by construction in a scene chosen by T2" (amendment 1). So each planner is swept over its own dial and compared as a curve.

  1. Pointsper arm (planner × dial): mean time_capped, mean min_gap, moving-collision rate over the common seeds
  2. Frontper planner and safety axis, dominated dial points dropped (safer never faster) so interpolation is well defined (amendment 2)
  3. Inside the frontinterpolate A's time at baseline point b's safety; A beats b if more than 2 s faster with the 95% paired interval excluding 0
  4. Outside the frontno extrapolation: only dominance (a point of A at least as safe on both axes and > 2 s faster), else "not comparable" = not beaten
  5. Both axes, every pointA beats planner B only if it beats every dial point of B on min_gap and on collision rate
  6. Holm across 4 baselinesp per baseline = worst point and axis; sorted, p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ 0.05 / (4 − i), stop at the first failure

A consequence stated in advance: "a baseline point faster than the primary's fastest arm can only be beaten by dominance" (amendment 2). With only two curve points after part 5, split's front is short (min_gap 0.158–0.232 m), so most baseline points were judged by dominance or were not comparable. The p per baseline is its worst point and axis: a "not comparable" pair has p = 1 (stop_go, cv m0.6, reactive m0.4 and m0.8), and where split is slower in every resample the p is also 1 (cv m0.2, cv m0.4, reactive m0.2, every vo dial). So all four baselines show Holm p = 1.0 in t5_primary.csv.

Holm–Bonferroni across the four baselines

The primary test is four comparisons (stop_go, constant velocity, reactive, velocity obstacles), corrected at 5% familywise with Holm's step-down procedure (amendment 1; curve_compare.main, --holm). Why Holm: it controls the familywise error like Bonferroni with more power. Reference: Holm 1979 [37]. Only the primary is corrected; the exploratory analyses are reported without correction and say so (amendment 2, (b); inloop_claimb.py docstring).

Margins, power and the number of seeds

  • The 2 s margin (D-14) is the smallest difference worth claiming, chosen as a detectability margin from the pilot's paired spread. It is "not an estimate of the headroom" (amendment 2); the oracle gate showed the headroom is smaller (time floor 17.74 s, vo w0.5 1.50 s above it).
  • Power calculation (D-14): pilot paired SDs (time 2.3–2.8 s; intrusion 0.4–1.6 s, from 10–33 pilot seeds), 80% power, 5% two-sided: 2 s needs 11–16 seeds, 1 s of intrusion 2–21, 0.5 s 7–84. So 40 confirmatory seeds (3000–3039), replacing GATES §2's "at least 100", which had "never been derived" (DECISIONS §1.4).
  • Detectable difference at the run size (part 5): with the T2 cell's median paired SD of 4.16 s, about 2.6 s at n = 20 and 1.84 s at n = 40.
  • Exploratory (a) margins (amendment 2): from baseline rows only (the T2 cell's 12 baseline arms, all 66 pairs, median paired SD: min_gap 0.356 m, moving-collision indicator 0.550), the detectable differences at 40 seeds are 0.158 m and 0.244. They were kept at 20 seeds, so (a) has less power (part 5).
  • Formula (our reconstruction; no power script is in the repo): δ = (z0.975 + z0.80) · SD / √n ≈ 2.80 · SD / √n. It reproduces 2.6 s (4.16, n 20), 1.84 s (n 40), 0.158 m (0.356, n 40), 0.244 (0.550, n 40), and D-14's 11–16 and 2–21 seeds as n = (2.80 · SD / δ)². D-14's 0.5 s row (7–84) is slightly above this normal approximation (6–81).
  • Why 20 seeds, not 40: split q0.20 on seed 3000 took 1,030 s (16.2 s per plan) with the CPU at 100% under ~35 Python processes, so one split process needed ~11 h, and the result had to be final that night (part 5). The cut was made before any learned result was read, except that one timing row, which was discarded (part 5).
  • The extension: the same arms and rules on seeds 3020–3039, declared at 02:20 before any of those results (3226e4f). It is done (RESULTS, 40-seed update, 70fd9c8; results/t5_primary_40.csv, results/inloop_claimb_40.csv): primary unchanged (beats no baseline); in-loop claim B after the switch +10.2 pts [+9.3, +11.2] coverage, interval score −1.33; split − lgbm_all time +1.24 s [+0.17, +2.34] at 40 seeds. Proper scores: interval score and pinball loss

Coverage alone is not a proper score: very wide boxes always cover (REFERENCES #20). We report a proper score next to every coverage number.

  • Interval score of a central (1 − α) interval [l, u] for truth y: (u − l) + (2/α)(l − y)·1[y < l] + (2/α)(y − u)·1[y > u], per axis, averaged over the two axes (tools/claim_a.py: interval_score; run_closed.py: inloop_stats uses 2/α = 20 for the 5–95% box). Gneiting & Raftery 2007 [31], Sec. 6.2: width plus a 2/α penalty on the miss distance, so misses cost more for higher-confidence intervals.
  • Pinball (quantile) loss ρq(d) = max(q·d, (q − 1)·d), d = y − prediction, averaged over levels and axes (foresight/core/eval.py: metrics; claim_a.score). The quantile-score family is proper for quantile forecasts (Gneiting & Raftery [31], Sec. 6.1; quantile regression from Koenker & Bassett 1978 [30]). GATES §2 lists pinball first among the metrics.
  • Width at matched coverage: GATES §2 metric 3, the honest comparison once both models are calibrated. live_variants_cp.py computes it with a post-hoc constant offset per variant giving 80% joint coverage.

Joint coverage and its nominal range

The robot plans around a 2D box, so we score the joint coverage of exactly that box, both axes at once (CTO-REVIEW §2.2). The 5–95% box per axis has a nominal joint coverage of 80–90%: 80% is the Bonferroni floor, 81% holds if the axes are independent, 90% if fully dependent (DEFENSE attack 5; RESULTS, claim A "Nominal coverage"). The 10–90% box has 60–80%. Per-person coverage is not scene safety: "with 16 people and 2 horizons, even perfect 80% boxes leave a high chance that at least one person is outside their box" (GATES §1); T5 has 24 people.

Online conformal offset

The box is widened or narrowed by one offset d learned from completed rows. Offline calibration table: the 10–90% box, target 80% joint, η 0.02 (D-13; RESULTS, calibration layer). WP-J2 and the loop spec: d ← d + η (miss − α) per completed row, α 0.2, η 0.02, on the raw 5–95% box (live_variants_cp.py docstring; amendment 2). In the loop: the batch rule d ← d + 0.05 (batch miss rate − 0.2) once per 2 s refresh (T4 part 1, chosen in part 4), labels from observed (filtered) positions, the planner box widened by the same d. Backing: adaptive conformal inference, Gibbs & Candès 2021 [17], which "provably achieves the desired coverage frequency over long-time intervals"; the guarantee is long-run average coverage, not per step, per person or conditional (REFERENCES #11). Part 1's reason for the batch rule: adaptive conformal assumes one update per time step, not 2,500 correlated rows per episode. Also on the list: Dixit et al. 2023 [16] (adaptive conformal for multistep predictions in MPC), Angelopoulos & Bates 2023 [18]. We claim no safety guarantee: Lindemann et al. 2023 [15] need the robot not to change the agents' behaviour (Assumption 1), and Huang et al. 2024 [32] argue calibration must depend on the plan (REFERENCES #12). The offline "on arrival" layer uses conformalised quantile regression with a joint score (foresight/core/conformal.py, RESULTS calibration section); CQR is Romano, Patterson & Candès 2019 [39].

The open-loop oracle as a reference, not an upper bound

run_closed.oracle_rows deep-copies the simulator at each replan, rolls it forward holding the current command, and gives the planner every person's true position at +0.4 s and at the planner horizons as zero-width boxes (D-21). People then react to the command actually chosen, so it knows the future under an action it may not take: "a strong reference for what better prediction could buy, not a strict upper bound" (DEFENSE attack 7; amendment 1). A per-candidate oracle would cost about 22× the simulation and was not built (DEFENSE attack 7). Its use as a gate: if even true futures can't beat the baselines by the margin, no learned predictor can show claim C in that scene (D-21).

Reading

The validation kept what it could defend: calibrated ranges in a new place (A, real data), adaptation after an unseen regime (B, simulation, offline and in the loop) and an offline intent layer on real data. On real pedestrians the live-context gain is small and direction-dependent (the real scenes have no sudden behaviour switch). It dropped what it could not defend: the f1 error story (withdrawn after the CV fix), claim C (three failed gates: T1, the oracle, the T5 primary) and the early Setting A design (no gain over CV). The TabPFN planner's slowness is not calibration: at q 0.15 split, frozen TabPFN and lgbm_all all take about 26 s, so the box planner's caution setting sets the speed (RESULTS, T5 (b); DEFENSE Q37). The Sunday exploratory tests (all with rules committed first) narrowed claim A (two scenes: direction-dependent; five scenes: not supported, a tie with LightGBM trained on about 15 times more varied data; data efficiency holds), widened claim B (in-loop gain over LightGBM in three crowds; adaptation on simulated cars), confirmed claim C's failure (dial sweep, two more crowds), measured claim D on an M3 Pro and showed stale prediction costs safety, and showed TabPFN can pick the move at its teacher's level on point estimates.

Known limits of the validation itself

  • 20 seeds for the learned arms. Detectable time difference ≈ 2.6 s (part 5), above the 2 s margin; exploratory (a) kept its 40-seed margins, so it has less power. Francis et al. recommend at least about 30 trials for real-robot runs (REFERENCES #21). The 40-seed extension is done (RESULTS, 70fd9c8): primary unchanged; in-loop claim B +10.2 pts [+9.3, +11.2], interval score −1.33; split − lgbm_all time +1.24 s [+0.17, +2.34].
  • One simulator, three crowds. Our own social-force crowd, with no validation against real trajectories (REFERENCES #14). The chosen cell qualifies by one seed (D-32); the two extra crowds (G1, G2) are exploratory, 20 seeds each, with mixed devices. Highway cars are a second simulator (highway-env), offline only. Erratic is a synthetic stress regime (DEFENSE Q18). The T2 bank's filter has r at the grid edge (amendment 2).
  • Five real scenes, one of them twice. Claim A was first tested on one transfer pair (ETH ↔ Hotel); the five-scene test adds Univ, Zara1 and Zara2, but Zara1 and Zara2 share a camera. Capped test samples, no robot in the real data, no closed loop on real data (RESULTS, calibration limits).
  • No human in the loop, no real robot. People react to the robot, but the boxes don't depend on the candidate move (GATES item 8 not built; DEFENSE Q31), so no conformal safety guarantee applies.
  • Latency 0 in T5. The 0.4 s arm was dropped from T5 (part 5) and run later for split only, on the L4 (§5 E17); baselines were never run with latency; the refresh delay was never implemented (amendment 1 correction); the replan and refit cadences (D-2, D-3) are untested.
  • Timings. All CPU timings are x64 emulated on an ARM laptop, often under contention (part 3: ~1.9 s per plan from 8 processes; part 5: 16.2 s per plan under ~35). The rented L4 run and Nooshin's M3 Pro smoke test (2 seeds, 20 s, not the T5 config) are the only GPU measurements; no embedded GPU was measured (DEPLOYMENT §2, docs/runs/mac_smoke.md). The pred_ms_per_plan in the L4 latency-arm files (750–860 ms) was measured under pod contention.
  • The closed loop is sensitive to 1 mm. Seed 3013 on the GPU: 25.4 s with a 0.47 m closest gap, against 25.8 s and 0.01 m on the laptop; "a 1 mm difference can flip which way the robot passes someone" (DEPLOYMENT §2). Single episodes don't reproduce across devices; only paired aggregates on one device mean anything.
  • The oracle is open-loop, so the gate bounds what prediction under the current command could buy, not what perfect prediction could (DEFENSE attack 7).
  • Selection on simulated data. The split design was chosen on T3's data (D-30), so T3 is selection-stage. T5 (b) on fresh seeds is the out-of-sample check, still simulated.
  • Rule and result in one commit. For the WP-J2 rule (306a2ab) and the intent gate (ed06094) the script and its output arrived together, so git can't show that the rule came first; the scripts print the rule before the numbers.
  • Dial range. The primary curve has two points (q 0.10, 0.15), slower than most baselines; the wider dial sweep (docs/EXPLORATORY_DIALS.md, seeds 3100–3119) is exploratory, its range chosen after T5, and also beat no baseline.
  • Multiplicity. Only the T5 primary and the curve tests (dial sweep, G1, G2: Holm over 4 baselines) are corrected. Claim A (two scenes and five scenes), T3, T5 (b), the crowd and car tests, the policy parity and the intent test report uncorrected 95% intervals.
  • Post-hoc checks. The H1 robustness check (docs/CEO-HANDOVER.md §4a) picked four LightGBM settings after seeing H1, one run each, with a scratch script not in the repo. The policy parity passes on point estimates; at 20 crowds the collision interval is wide ([−15, +25] pts).
  • LightGBM not validation-tuned (D-15); the smoothing test ran at margin 0.4, not 0.2 (part 2); the T1 intervals are marginal, not paired (DEFENSE Q12).
