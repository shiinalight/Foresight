05bValidation: what we tested, how, and why

This section is the validation methodology in one place: what each claim says, which test could have proved it wrong, the order in which the tests ran (cheap offline gates first, the closed loop last), the statistics and why we chose them, every KPI we track and where it is computed, how we kept the comparisons fair, what came out, and the limits of the validation itself. Every number names its source file and section. Where two sources disagree, the last subsection lists it rather than picking one silently.

Statistical methods, and why each

Paired seeds (common random numbers)

Every planner sees the same crowds: same seed, same start, goal and people (DEFENSE Q12; run_closed.py runs seed-outer, planner-inner, amendment 2). curve_compare.load() keeps only seeds present in every arm, so a run cut short still gives paired comparisons. Why: crowd-to-crowd variation is much larger than planner-to-planner differences (pilot paired time SDs 2.3–2.8 s, D-14), and pairing removes the shared part. Backing: REFERENCES #21 says plainly that no paper we checked prescribes paired seeds: "That is standard common-random-numbers practice and our own design choice, so state it as a choice." Francis et al. 2025 [25] recommend comparing policies on the same scenarios. The T1 intervals were marginal per configuration, not paired, which is conservative (DEFENSE Q12).

Person-level bootstrap (offline)

Intervals on offline differences resample whole people, not rows. Why: "rows 0.4 s apart are near-copies" (docs/CTO-REVIEW.md §3), so a row bootstrap would treat one person's 20 near-identical rows as 20 independent observations and give intervals that are far too narrow. GATES §2 requires it. How: tools/claim_a_boot.py computes the per-row difference (TabPFN minus LightGBM, averaged over the 5 context seeds), sums it per person (episode, agent_id), draws persons with replacement 5,000 times and takes the ratio of summed differences to summed row counts; 95% percentile interval. The intent gate uses the same scheme with 2,000 draws (tools/intent_layer.py docstring). Agarwal et al. 2021 [33] back bootstrap interval estimates in general; REFERENCES notes they use a stratified bootstrap over runs and tasks, not ours.

Paired-seed bootstrap (closed loop)

Seeds are resampled with replacement, the same draw for every arm, and the whole comparison is recomputed per draw: 2,000 draws in curve_compare.compare(), 5,000 in inloop_claimb.py; 95% percentile intervals. In curve_compare, a draw in which the comparison can't be made (the baseline point falls outside the front and no dominance exists) counts as −∞, i.e. against the planner under test. The one-sided p-value is the share of draws with difference ≤ 0.

Matched-safety curves on the Pareto front

A single operating point can't separate "safer" from "slower" (CTO-REVIEW §3), and the original absolute rule (≤ 2% collisions, ≤ 1 s intrusion) "disqualifies baselines by construction in a scene chosen by T2" (amendment 1). So each planner is swept over its own dial and compared as a curve.

  1. Pointsper arm (planner × dial): mean time_capped, mean min_gap, moving-collision rate over the common seeds
  2. Frontper planner and safety axis, dominated dial points dropped (safer never faster) so interpolation is well defined (amendment 2)
  3. Inside the frontinterpolate A's time at baseline point b's safety; A beats b if more than 2 s faster with the 95% paired interval excluding 0
  4. Outside the frontno extrapolation: only dominance (a point of A at least as safe on both axes and > 2 s faster), else "not comparable" = not beaten
  5. Both axes, every pointA beats planner B only if it beats every dial point of B on min_gap and on collision rate
  6. Holm across 4 baselinesp per baseline = worst point and axis; sorted, p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ 0.05 / (4 − i), stop at the first failure

A consequence stated in advance: "a baseline point faster than the primary's fastest arm can only be beaten by dominance" (amendment 2). With only two curve points after part 5, split's front is short (min_gap 0.158–0.232 m), so most baseline points were judged by dominance or were not comparable. Every "not comparable" pair carries p = 1, which is why all four baselines show Holm p = 1.0 in t5_primary.csv.

Holm–Bonferroni across the four baselines

The primary test is four comparisons (stop_go, constant velocity, reactive, velocity obstacles), corrected at 5% familywise with Holm's step-down procedure (amendment 1; curve_compare.main, --holm). Why Holm: it controls the familywise error like Bonferroni with more power. Reference: Holm 1979 is not in REFERENCES.md. Only the primary is corrected; the exploratory analyses are reported without correction and say so (amendment 2, (b); inloop_claimb.py docstring).

Margins, power and the number of seeds

  • The 2 s margin (D-14) is the smallest difference worth claiming, chosen as a detectability margin from the pilot's paired spread. It is "not an estimate of the headroom" (amendment 2); the oracle gate showed the headroom is smaller (time floor 17.74 s, vo w0.5 1.50 s above it).
  • Power calculation (D-14): pilot paired SDs (time 2.3–2.8 s; intrusion 0.4–1.6 s, from 10–33 pilot seeds), 80% power, 5% two-sided: 2 s needs 11–16 seeds, 1 s of intrusion 2–21, 0.5 s 7–84. So 40 confirmatory seeds (3000–3039), replacing GATES §2's "at least 100", which had "never been derived" (DECISIONS §1.4).
  • Detectable difference at the run size (part 5): with the T2 cell's median paired SD of 4.16 s, about 2.6 s at n = 20 and 1.84 s at n = 40.
  • Exploratory (a) margins (amendment 2): from baseline rows only (the T2 cell's 12 baseline arms, all 66 pairs, median paired SD: min_gap 0.356 m, moving-collision indicator 0.550), the detectable differences at 40 seeds are 0.158 m and 0.244. They were kept at 20 seeds, so (a) has less power (part 5).
  • Formula (our reconstruction; no power script is in the repo): δ = (z0.975 + z0.80) · SD / √n ≈ 2.80 · SD / √n. It reproduces 2.6 s (4.16, n 20), 1.84 s (n 40), 0.158 m (0.356, n 40), 0.244 (0.550, n 40), and D-14's 11–16 and 2–21 seeds as n = (2.80 · SD / δ)². D-14's 0.5 s row (7–84) is slightly above this normal approximation (6–81).
  • Why 20 seeds, not 40: split q0.20 on seed 3000 took 1,030 s (16.2 s per plan) with the CPU at 100% under ~35 Python processes, so one split process needed ~11 h, and the result had to be final that night (part 5). The cut was made before any learned result was read, except that one timing row, which was discarded (part 5).
  • The extension: the same arms and rules on seeds 3020–3039, declared at 02:20 before any of those results (3226e4f); running when this page was written, not analysed here. The 20-seed analysis stays the confirmatory result (RESULTS, T5).

Proper scores: interval score and pinball loss

Coverage alone is not a proper score: very wide boxes always cover (REFERENCES #20). We report a proper score next to every coverage number.

  • Interval score of a central (1 − α) interval [l, u] for truth y: (u − l) + (2/α)(l − y)·1[y < l] + (2/α)(y − u)·1[y > u], per axis, averaged over the two axes (tools/claim_a.py: interval_score; run_closed.py: inloop_stats uses 2/α = 20 for the 5–95% box). Gneiting & Raftery 2007 [31], Sec. 6.2: width plus a 2/α penalty on the miss distance, so misses cost more for higher-confidence intervals.
  • Pinball (quantile) loss ρq(d) = max(q·d, (q − 1)·d), d = y − prediction, averaged over levels and axes (foresight/core/eval.py: metrics; claim_a.score). The quantile-score family is proper for quantile forecasts (Gneiting & Raftery [31], Sec. 6.1; quantile regression from Koenker & Bassett 1978 [30]). GATES §2 lists pinball first among the metrics.
  • Width at matched coverage: GATES §2 metric 3, the honest comparison once both models are calibrated. live_variants_cp.py computes it with a post-hoc constant offset per variant giving 80% joint coverage.

Joint coverage and its nominal range

The robot plans around a 2D box, so we score the joint coverage of exactly that box, both axes at once (CTO-REVIEW §2.2). The 5–95% box per axis has a nominal joint coverage of 80–90%: 80% is the Bonferroni floor, 81% holds if the axes are independent, 90% if fully dependent (DEFENSE attack 5; RESULTS, claim A "Nominal coverage"). The 10–90% box has 60–80%. Per-person coverage is not scene safety: with 24 people and 2 horizons, some person is likely outside their box at any moment (GATES §1).

Online conformal offset

The box is widened or narrowed by one offset d learned from completed rows. Offline: d ← d + η (miss − α) per completed row, α 0.2, η 0.02, on the raw 5–95% box (D-13; amendment 2 spec). In the loop: the batch rule d ← d + 0.05 (batch miss rate − 0.2) once per 2 s refresh (T4 part 1, chosen in part 4), labels from observed (filtered) positions, the planner box widened by the same d. Backing: adaptive conformal inference, Gibbs & Candès 2021 [17], which "provably achieves the desired coverage frequency over long-time intervals"; the guarantee is long-run average coverage, not per step, per person or conditional (REFERENCES #11). Part 1's reason for the batch rule: adaptive conformal assumes one update per time step, not 2,500 correlated rows per episode. Also on the list: Dixit et al. 2023 [16] (adaptive conformal for multistep predictions in MPC), Angelopoulos & Bates 2023 [18]. We claim no safety guarantee: Lindemann et al. 2023 [15] need the robot not to change the agents' behaviour (Assumption 1), and Huang et al. 2024 [32] argue calibration must depend on the plan (REFERENCES #12). The offline "on arrival" layer uses conformalised quantile regression with a joint score (foresight/core/conformal.py, RESULTS calibration section); no CQR paper is in REFERENCES.

The open-loop oracle as a reference, not an upper bound

run_closed.oracle_rows deep-copies the simulator at each replan, rolls it forward holding the current command, and gives the planner every person's true position at +0.4 s and at the planner horizons as zero-width boxes (D-21). People then react to the command actually chosen, so it knows the future under an action it may not take: "a strong reference for what better prediction could buy, not a strict upper bound" (DEFENSE attack 7; amendment 1). A per-candidate oracle would cost about 22× the simulation and was not built (DEFENSE attack 7). Its use as a gate: if even true futures can't beat the baselines by the margin, no learned predictor can show claim C in that scene (D-21).

Reading

The validation kept what it could defend: calibrated ranges in a new place (A, real data), adaptation after an unseen regime (B, simulation, offline and in the loop) and an offline intent layer on real data. It dropped what it could not: the f1 error story (withdrawn after the CV fix), claim C (three failed gates: T1, the oracle, the T5 primary) and live context on real data (Setting A, no gain over CV). Why the TabPFN planner is slower: the planner uses only box width, so honest, wider boxes after the switch veto more moves (RESULTS, T5; DEFENSE Q37).

Known limits of the validation itself

  • 20 seeds for the learned arms. Detectable time difference ≈ 2.6 s (part 5), above the 2 s margin; exploratory (a) kept its 40-seed margins, so it has less power. Francis et al. recommend at least about 30 trials for real-robot runs (REFERENCES #21). The 40-seed extension is running and not analysed here.
  • One simulator, one cell. Our own social-force crowd, with no validation against real trajectories (REFERENCES #14). The chosen cell qualifies by one seed (D-32). Erratic is a synthetic stress regime (DEFENSE Q18). The T2 bank's filter has r at the grid edge (amendment 2).
  • Two real scenes. One transfer pair (ETH ↔ Hotel), capped test samples (900 rows per direction), no robot in the real data, no closed loop on real data (RESULTS, calibration limits). Claim B on real data with the final design is still pending.
  • No human in the loop, no real robot. People react to the robot, but the boxes don't depend on the candidate move (GATES item 8 not built; DEFENSE Q31), so no conformal safety guarantee applies.
  • Latency 0 in T5, and no latency run at all. The 0.4 s arm was dropped (part 5); the refresh delay was never implemented (amendment 1 correction); the replan and refit cadences (D-2, D-3) are untested.
  • Timings. All CPU timings are x64 emulated on an ARM laptop, often under contention (part 3: ~1.9 s per plan from 8 processes; part 5: 16.2 s per plan under ~35). The rented L4 run is the only GPU measurement; no embedded GPU was measured (DEPLOYMENT §2).
  • The closed loop is sensitive to 1 mm. Seed 3013 on the GPU: 25.4 s with a 0.47 m closest gap, against 25.8 s and 0.01 m on the laptop; "a 1 mm difference can flip which way the robot passes someone" (DEPLOYMENT §2). Single episodes don't reproduce across devices; only paired aggregates on one device mean anything.
  • The oracle is open-loop, so the gate bounds what prediction under the current command could buy, not what perfect prediction could (DEFENSE attack 7).
  • Selection on simulated data. The split design was chosen on T3's data (D-30), so T3 is selection-stage. T5 (b) on fresh seeds is the out-of-sample check, still simulated.
  • Rule and result in one commit. For the WP-J2 rule (306a2ab) and the intent gate (ed06094) the script and its output arrived together, so git can't show that the rule came first; the scripts print the rule before the numbers.
  • Multiplicity. Only the T5 primary is corrected. Claim A (2 directions × 2 comparators × 3 metrics), T3, T5 (b) and the intent test report uncorrected 95% intervals.
  • LightGBM not validation-tuned (D-15); the smoothing test ran at margin 0.4, not 0.2 (part 2); the T1 intervals are marginal, not paired (DEFENSE Q12).

Where the sources disagree (found while writing this section)

Listed, not fixed. The page above follows the result files.

  • RESULTS T5, "dominated (slower and no safer)". It says this of vo w0.5, w1, w2 and cv m0.2. In t5_primary_points.csv split is safer than all four on both axes (e.g. split q0.15 0.158 m / 15% against vo w0.5 −0.038 m / 40%, cv m0.2 0.089 m / 25%). The CSV's how = dominance names the comparison branch (a split point at least as safe on both axes exists), and its negative difference means split is slower (3.92–6.15 s). The correct reading is "safer but slower, so not beaten", as PLAN §2 C and DEFENSE Q32 put it.
  • Test count. The brief said 126 tests in test_robustness_*.py. Those three files collect 104; all of tests/ collects 127, of which 126 pass and 1 is a strict xfail (docs/MAC_SETUP.md: "127 … 1 expected failure").
  • "Tuned" LightGBM. RESULTS calls m50:n100 "tuned for small data"; it is one regularised setting, and the GATES §2 validation grid was never run (D-15).
  • Oracle as upper bound. run_closed.py (comment at the oracle branch) says "upper bound on prediction"; D-21, amendment 1 and DEFENSE attack 7 say it is not one.
  • D-21 axes. The gate text names "time-vs-intrusion" curves; amendment 1 and curve_compare.py ran it on min_gap and moving-collision rate.
  • S1 gate. GATES §1 defines it as filtered velocity error plus parity test B8; it was run as "CV within 10% of the published CVM" (D-26), a comparison GATES §2 lists under "not allowed". PLAN §3 S1 says "within 3–14%", D-26 says "within 10%" (the difference is the window rule, DEFENSE attack 10).
  • WP-J2 burden of proof. The rule printed by live_variants_cp.py is "primary = frozen2k+offset, UNLESS split2k+offset beats it"; D-30 and the T3 rule as quoted elsewhere say split is primary unless frozen+offset matches it. Same outcome here, since split won on both criteria.
